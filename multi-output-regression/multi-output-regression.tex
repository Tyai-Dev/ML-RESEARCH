\begin{abstract}
The regression counterpart of softmax's Bernoulli $\to$ multinoulli step:
a vector response $y \in \mathbb{R}^K$ that is conditionally multivariate
normal around a linear map of $x$. Unlike softmax, the closed form
survives --- the Frobenius loss separates over outputs into $K$ ordinary
least-squares problems sharing one Gram factorization --- and this note
records why, when correlated noise still doesn't change the answer, and
what the one-hot ``linearization trick'' for classification gets right
and wrong. Demonstrations in \texttt{multioutput.py}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The model}

\begin{equation}
  y \mid x \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(W^\top x,\; \sigma^2 I_K\bigr),
  \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times K},
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
one weight column per output. The conditional NLL, up to constants, is
the Frobenius least-squares loss
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(W) \;=\; \frac{1}{2n}\,\lVert XW - Y\rVert_F^2
  \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{2n}\,\lVert Xw_k - Y_{:,k}\rVert^2 ,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
and the second equality is the whole story: \emph{the loss separates over
output columns}. Each $w_k$ solves an ordinary least-squares problem
against the same design.

\begin{proposition}
If $X$ has full column rank, the unique MLE is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\widehat{W} \;=\; (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top Y ,\;}
  \label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
the normal equations of the linear-regression note with $K$ right-hand
sides --- one Gram factorization, reused for every output.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\nabla_W \mathcal{L} = \tfrac1n X^\top (XW - Y) = 0$ gives
$X^\top X\, W = X^\top Y$; column $k$ of this system is exactly the
single-output normal equation for $(X, Y_{:,k})$, with the usual strict
convexity giving uniqueness.
\end{proof}

Per sample, the gradient is $x_i\,(x_i^\top W - y_i^\top)$ --- residual
(now a $K$-vector) times features, the same GLM shape as everywhere else
in this collection; the script's SGD and autograd routes verify it by
trajectory identity.

\begin{remark}[Correlated noise changes nothing --- here]
Replace $\sigma^2 I_K$ in \eqref{eq:model} by a full covariance
$\Sigma$: the NLL becomes a $\Sigma^{-1}$-weighted quadratic, yet its
minimizer is still \eqref{eq:closed}. Reason: the stationarity condition
reads $X^\top (XW - Y)\,\Sigma^{-1} = 0$, and $\Sigma^{-1}$ is
invertible, so it drops out. This is the seemingly-unrelated-regressions
collapse: correlated outputs only matter for estimation once the outputs
use \emph{different} regressors; with a shared design, per-column OLS is
already the MLE. (Estimating $\Sigma$ itself: the $1/n$ residual
covariance, as in the Gaussian note.)
\end{remark}

\section{The one-hot linearization trick}

Multi-output regression tempts a shortcut for $K$-class classification:
encode labels as one-hot vectors $Y \in \{0,1\}^{n\times K}$, solve
\eqref{eq:closed}, classify by $\arg\max_k$ of the fitted scores. What
this gets \emph{right}: linear score functions and often reasonable
accuracy (the script measures it a few points below the softmax fit on
the same data). What it gets \emph{wrong}: the scores are not
probabilities --- the script's histogram shows fitted values well outside
$[0,1]$ (they need not sum to 1 either), because a Gaussian likelihood on
$\{0,1\}$ targets is misspecified. Squared loss also penalizes
\emph{over}-confident correct scores, dragging boundaries toward outlying
but well-classified points. The proper likelihood for a categorical
response is the multinoulli --- softmax regression --- which is exactly
the difference between modeling $\mathbb{E}[Y|x]$ with the wrong noise
and modeling $\Pr(Y|x)$ itself.

\section{Experiment}

$n = 3000$, $d = 3$, $K = 2$, noise $\sigma = 0.8$ (seed 7): the closed
form recovers $W^\ast$ to sampling accuracy; per-column OLS reproduces it
column by column (asserted); GD converges to it; hand and autograd SGD
trajectories coincide to $10^{-10}$. The one-hot trick on 3-class softmax
data classifies within a few points of the Bayes rule while its
``probabilities'' span roughly $[-0.4, 1.3]$ --- linear scores, broken
semantics.

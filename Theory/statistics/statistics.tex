\section{Estimators and their quality}

An \emph{estimator} is any function $\hat\theta = T(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ of the
sample, targeting a parameter $\theta$. Its basic report card:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{bias}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta -
  \mathbb{E}\hat\theta)^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
combined by the decomposition (expand the square around
$\mathbb{E}\hat\theta$; the cross term vanishes):
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta)
  \;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\bigr]
  \;=\; \mathrm{bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var} .\;}
  \label{eq:msedecomp}
\end{equation}
\emph{Consistency}: $\hat\theta_n \to \theta$ in probability as
$n\to\infty$ --- the LLN in estimator's clothing.

\begin{remark}[Unbiasedness is a choice, not a virtue]
The Gaussian variance MLE $\tfrac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ has
$\mathbb{E} = \tfrac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$ (biased); Bessel's
$\tfrac{1}{n-1}$ fixes the bias --- yet the script measures the
\emph{biased} estimator to have the lower MSE at small $n$. Trading a
little bias for less variance is often the better deal
\eqref{eq:msedecomp}; regularization is this trade, industrialized.
\end{remark}

\section{Where estimators come from: MLE and MAP}

Section 1 grades estimators; this section is where the two canonical
ones come from. Both start from the same object.

\begin{definition}[Likelihood]
Fix the observed sample $x_{1:n}$ and read the joint density as a
function of the \emph{parameter}:
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i;\theta).
  \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
$L$ is not a probability distribution over $\theta$ --- it need not
integrate to one in $\theta$. It is a scoring of parameter values by
how well they explain what was actually seen.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Maximum likelihood}

\begin{definition}[MLE]
\begin{equation}
  \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\, L(\theta)
  \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\, \ell(\theta),
  \qquad
  \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln f(x_i;\theta).
  \label{eq:mledef}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Passing to $\ell = \ln L$ changes nothing about the maximizer ($\ln$ is
strictly increasing) and everything about the algebra: the product
becomes a sum, each term differentiates by the log-derivative rule
(derivations note), and numerically a sum of $n$ log-densities does not
underflow the way a product of $n$ densities does.

\paragraph{The solving procedure.} Always the same four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write $\ell(\theta)$, collapsing the sum into the sufficient
    statistics (Section 3) --- the data enters only through them.
  \item Differentiate in $\theta$. If the parameters are constrained
    (probabilities on a simplex), maximize the Lagrangian instead
    (optimization note).
  \item Solve the stationarity equation $\nabla \ell = 0$.
  \item Certify: strict concavity of $\ell$ makes the stationary point
    the unique global maximum; otherwise check the Hessian and the
    boundary. (For Gaussians, concavity holds in the right
    parameterization --- the precision, not the covariance.)
\end{enumerate}
The distributions note executes these four steps verbatim for
Bernoulli, categorical, and both Gaussians.

Why maximum likelihood is \emph{the} default is this page's later
sections: the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal, and
asymptotically efficient \eqref{eq:mleasymp} --- in the limit, nothing
does better. Two more properties worth naming now: \emph{invariance}
(the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$), and the
information-theoretic reading --- maximizing $\ell$ is minimizing the
cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and the model, i.e.\
minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat p_{\mathrm{data}} \,\Vert\,
p_\theta)$ (information-theory note): MLE fits the model distribution
to the data distribution, which is why every loss in the LLM track is
an NLL.

\subsection{Maximum a posteriori}

MAP treats $\theta$ itself as random, with a \emph{prior} density
$\pi(\theta)$ encoding what is believed before seeing data. Bayes'
theorem updates it:
\begin{equation}
  \pi(\theta \mid x_{1:n})
  \;=\; \frac{L(\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}
             {\int L(\theta')\,\pi(\theta')\,d\theta'} .
  \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[MAP]
\begin{equation}
  \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}
  \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\, \pi(\theta \mid x_{1:n})
  \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\,\bigl[\ell(\theta) + \ln \pi(\theta)\bigr].
  \label{eq:mapdef}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The denominator of \eqref{eq:bayes} does not involve $\theta$, so it
drops from the $\arg\max$ --- MAP never computes the (usually
intractable) evidence integral. What remains is the MLE's objective
plus one additive term: \emph{the same calculus, with $\ln\pi$ along
for the ride}. Every MAP derivation is the corresponding MLE derivation
with modified constants.

\begin{definition}[Conjugate prior]
$\pi$ is \emph{conjugate} to the likelihood if the posterior
\eqref{eq:bayes} belongs to the same family as $\pi$. Then updating is
pure bookkeeping on the family's parameters:
Beta$\to$Beta for Bernoulli counts, Dirichlet$\to$Dirichlet for
categorical counts, Gaussian$\to$Gaussian for a Gaussian mean,
inverse-Gamma (inverse-Wishart) $\to$ same for a variance (covariance).
\end{definition}

Conjugacy is what makes MAP solutions closed-form, and it gives them a
uniform reading: the prior acts as \emph{pseudo-observations}. A
Beta$(\alpha,\beta)$ prior is $\alpha{-}1$ imaginary successes and
$\beta{-}1$ imaginary failures added to the tally; a Gaussian prior on
a mean is an imaginary sample at $\mu_0$ whose weight is the prior
precision; add-one (Laplace) smoothing of the categorical MLE is
exactly the Dirichlet-MAP with $\alpha_j = 2$. As $n$ grows the real
data outvotes the imaginary --- $\ell$ scales with $n$ while $\ln\pi$
stays fixed --- so
$\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}} \to \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}$, and with a
flat prior ($\pi \equiv$ const on the parameter space) they coincide at
every $n$.

\begin{remark}[MAP is regularization]
For a model with weights $w$, take
$\pi(w) = \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)$: then
$\ln\pi(w) = -\tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert w \rVert_2^2 + \text{const}$,
and \eqref{eq:mapdef} is empirical risk with an L2 penalty --- ridge
regression, weight decay. A Laplace prior gives
$-\lambda \lVert w\rVert_1$ --- lasso. Every regularized loss in this
repository is a MAP estimate whose prior is stated by the penalty.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[One honest asymmetry]
The MLE is invariant under reparameterization; the MAP is \emph{not}
(the prior density picks up a Jacobian when $\theta$ is transformed,
which can move the posterior's peak). The price of injecting beliefs is
that the answer depends on the coordinates in which they were stated.
\end{remark}

\section{Sufficiency}

\begin{definition}
$T(x_{1:n})$ is \emph{sufficient} for $\theta$ if the conditional
distribution of the sample given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$ ---
once you know $T$, the rest of the data carries no further information
about $\theta$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fisher--Neyman factorization]
$T$ is sufficient for $\theta$ iff the likelihood factors as
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta; x_{1:n}) \;=\; g\bigl(T(x_{1:n}), \theta\bigr)\,h(x_{1:n}),
  \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
with $h$ free of $\theta$.
\end{theorem}

Read off our worked examples: Bernoulli
$L = p^m(1-p)^{n-m}$ --- take $T=m$, $h\equiv 1$; Gaussian --- expanding
the exponent leaves $\theta$ multiplying only $\sum x_i$ and
$\sum x_i^2$, so that pair is sufficient. This is why both MLEs are
formulas in one or two summaries of the data rather than the data itself.
(Rao--Blackwell sharpens the point: conditioning any unbiased estimator on
a sufficient statistic never increases its variance --- good estimators
factor through $T$.)

\section{Fisher information and the Cram\'er--Rao bound}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{score} is $\partial_\theta \log f(x;\theta)$; it has mean zero.
The \emph{Fisher information} per sample is its variance,
\begin{equation}
  I(\theta)
  \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(\partial_\theta \log f(x;\theta)\bigr)^2\Bigr]
  \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\partial_\theta^2 \log f(x;\theta)\bigr],
  \label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
the second form under regularity. For $n$ i.i.d.\ samples information
adds: $I_n = n I(\theta)$.
\end{definition}

Intuition: $I$ measures how sharply the log-likelihood peaks --- how
loudly one sample speaks about $\theta$. Bernoulli:
$I(p) = \tfrac{1}{p(1-p)}$ (extreme $p$ are \emph{more} informative per
sample). Gaussian mean: $I(\mu) = 1/\sigma^2$.

\begin{theorem}[Cram\'er--Rao]
For any \emph{unbiased} estimator $\hat\theta$ under regularity
conditions,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \;\ge\; \frac{1}{n\,I(\theta)} .\;}
  \label{eq:crlb}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

An estimator achieving \eqref{eq:crlb} is \emph{efficient}. The Bernoulli
MLE $\hat p = \bar x$ is exactly efficient:
$\mathrm{Var}(\bar x) = p(1-p)/n = 1/(nI(p))$ --- the script's second
panel shows the empirical variance sitting on the bound at every $n$.

\section{Asymptotics of the MLE}

Under regularity, the MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal:
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{n}\,\bigl(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}} - \theta\bigr)
  \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\;
  \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; I(\theta)^{-1}\bigr),
  \label{eq:mleasymp}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the MLE attains the Cram\'er--Rao limit \emph{asymptotically} even
when no finite-$n$ efficient estimator exists --- the deep reason maximum
likelihood is the default: nothing does better in the limit. Two
practical corollaries: standard errors
$\approx \sqrt{1/(n\hat I)}$, and the invariance property (the MLE of
$g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$) used by every reparameterization in our
experiments.

\title{Bernoulli Maximum-Likelihood Estimation, Three Ways:\\

\begin{abstract}
We estimate the parameter of a Bernoulli distribution from i.i.d.\ samples
three ways: the theoretical (closed-form) maximum-likelihood solution;
stochastic gradient descent with a gradient derived by hand; and stochastic
gradient descent with the gradient computed by PyTorch's autograd. The
companion script \texttt{mle.py} runs all three and verifies, by driving
the two SGD variants through identical batch sequences, that autograd
reproduces the hand-derived gradient to floating-point precision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}

Let $x_1,\dots,x_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ with
$x_i \in \{0,1\}$ and unknown $p \in (0,1)$. The likelihood of the sample
and the average negative log-likelihood (NLL) are
\begin{equation}
  L(p) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}(p) \;=\; -\xbar\log p - (1-\xbar)\log(1-p),
  \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
where $\xbar = \tfrac1n\sum_i x_i$. The data enters $\mathcal{L}$ only
through $\xbar$: the sample mean is a \emph{sufficient statistic} for $p$.
Maximizing $L$ and minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ are equivalent because
$-\tfrac1n\log(\cdot)$ is strictly decreasing.

\section{Route 1: the theoretical solution}

\begin{proposition}
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \xbar$, and it is the unique minimizer of
$\mathcal{L}$ on $(0,1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Stationarity: $\mathcal{L}'(p) = -\xbar/p + (1-\xbar)/(1-p) = 0$ gives
$p(1-\xbar) = (1-p)\xbar$, hence $p = \xbar$. Uniqueness:
$\mathcal{L}''(p) = \xbar/p^{2} + (1-\xbar)/(1-p)^{2} > 0$, so
$\mathcal{L}$ is strictly convex on $(0,1)$ and the stationary point is the
global minimum.
\end{proof}

\section{Route 2: SGD with a hand-derived gradient}

Gradient methods should search an unconstrained space, but $p$ lives in
$(0,1)$. Parameterize
\begin{equation}
  p = \sigmoid(t) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R},
\end{equation}
which is legitimate by the \emph{invariance} of maximum likelihood: if
$\hat{t}$ maximizes the likelihood over $t$, then $\sigmoid(\hat{t})$
maximizes it over $p$.

For a mini-batch $B \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$ with mean $\xbar_B$, substituting
$p=\sigmoid(t)$ into \eqref{eq:nll} and simplifying with
$\log\sigmoid(t) = -\log(1+e^{-t})$ and
$\log(1-\sigmoid(t)) = -t-\log(1+e^{-t})$ gives the batch objective in
logit form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_B(t) \;=\; \log\!\left(1+e^{t}\right) - \xbar_B\, t ,
  \label{eq:logitnll}
\end{equation}
which is convex in $t$ (its second derivative is
$\sigmoid(t)(1-\sigmoid(t)) > 0$). Differentiating, everything collapses
through $\sigmoid' = \sigmoid(1-\sigmoid)$ to the remarkably clean
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\mathcal{L}_B}{dt} \;=\; \sigmoid(t) - \xbar_B .
  \label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
SGD iterates $t \leftarrow t - \eta\,(\sigmoid(t) - \xbar_B)$ over
reshuffled mini-batches: each update pushes the current estimate
$\sigmoid(t)$ toward the batch mean, and the fixed point of the averaged
dynamics is $\sigmoid(t^{\ast}) = \xbar$ --- the closed form again.

\paragraph{Polyak--Ruppert averaging.}
With a constant step size, the SGD iterate never converges: it reaches a
stationary \emph{distribution}, a noise ball around the optimum whose
radius scales with $\eta$ and the batch variance. The estimator is
therefore taken as the average of the final epoch's iterates,
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{SGD}} = \tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k} \sigmoid(t_k)$, which
suppresses the oscillation and lands within a few $10^{-3}$ of $\xbar$.

\section{Route 3: SGD with autograd}

PyTorch's \texttt{binary\_cross\_entropy\_with\_logits}$(t, x_B)$ computes
exactly the batch objective \eqref{eq:logitnll}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BCE}(t, x_B)
  \;=\; \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\Bigl[\log\!\left(1+e^{t}\right) - x_i t\Bigr]
  \;=\; \mathcal{L}_B(t).
\end{equation}
Calling \texttt{loss.backward()} applies reverse-mode automatic
differentiation to this expression, so it must produce
\texttt{t.grad} $= \sigmoid(t)-\xbar_B$, identically \eqref{eq:grad} ---
autograd mechanizes the chain rule; it does not change the mathematics.
\texttt{torch.optim.SGD} then performs the same update
$t \leftarrow t - \eta\,\texttt{t.grad}$.

\paragraph{Proof by computation.}
The script drives routes 2 and 3 through the \emph{identical} sequence of
mini-batches, the same $\eta$, and the same initialization $t_0 = 0$ (in
\texttt{float64} on both sides). Under those conditions the two
trajectories can differ only if the gradients differ; the script asserts
$\max_k |\sigmoid(t_k^{\text{hand}}) - \sigmoid(t_k^{\text{autograd}})| <
10^{-10}$, which passes --- an end-to-end check that
\eqref{eq:grad} is exactly what autograd computes.

\section{Experiment}

With $p = 0.3$ and $n = 5000$ (seed 7): the closed form gives
$\hat{p} = \xbar \approx 0.2978$; both SGD routes, Polyak-averaged over the
last epoch, land within a few $10^{-3}$ of it; and the two SGD trajectories
coincide to $10^{-10}$. The left panel of the figure produced by
\texttt{mle.py} shows the strictly convex NLL landscape with all estimates
on it; the right panel overlays the two (indistinguishable) trajectories
settling into the noise ball around $\xbar$.

Three routes, one destination: the likelihood surface --- not the
optimizer --- decides where estimation lands.
